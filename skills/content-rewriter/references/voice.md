# The voice

A writing style for website content, on any subject. It was distilled from a set of articles with strong
read and conversion figures; what is kept here is how they were written, not what they were about. The
subject, audience, facts and calls to action all come from the content you are given.

Examples in this file show sentence shapes only. Their details are made up. Never carry a name, number or
claim from an example into real copy.

## 1. Who is speaking

**An insider talking to their own kind.** Not an organisation addressing customers. The writer sits on the
reader's side of the table: someone who has done the thing, had the problem, or works in the trade, talking
to people in the same position.

So **"we" does two jobs**: we-the-people-in-this-situation ("we've all had the letter") and we-the-team
("we fixed it in a week"). Use both. Across an article, "we/our/us" and "you/your" should come out roughly
level. Copy that is all "we" is a brochure; copy that is all "you" is a lecture.

Only claim shared experience the project profile supports. If the organisation is not made up of its own
audience, drop the first kind of "we" and keep the tone: an expert who is plainly on the reader's side.

## 2. Tone

- **Straight-talking and confident.** Say the uncomfortable thing plainly, including the downside of what
  is on offer. Honesty about cost, limits or trade-offs is a selling point, because the reader already
  suspects them.
- **On the reader's side, sharp about what is squeezing them.** Share their frustration with the system,
  the rules, the industry habit or the bad advice, sometimes with dry humour. Aim it at decisions and
  practices, never at named individuals and never at a vulnerable group.
- **Energetic, not hysterical.** Urgency comes from real deadlines and real numbers, not adjectives or
  capitals. At most one exclamation mark in a piece.
- **Conversational idiom**, the kind the audience uses themselves, a few per piece and never a pile-up.
  Use the idiom of the site's own country.

## 3. Rhythm

- **Short paragraphs.** Median around 17 words; about two-thirds at 25 words or fewer. One- and
  two-sentence paragraphs are normal.
- **Sentences** around 20 words at the median, with deliberate short punches. *"It worked."* *"That was
  the whole problem."*
- **Question, then answer.** The signature move, two to four times in an article:
  - *"Their biggest worry? Losing the customers they already had."*
  - *"And the paperwork? Done in an afternoon."*
  - *"Why? Because nobody had asked."*
- **Contractions throughout**: we're, it's, you've, don't.
- **The site's own English.** British spelling, £ and "1 May 2026" dates unless the profile says otherwise.

## 4. Headlines

Newspaper-style, built to be clicked. Pick one shape:

- **A quote from a real person:** *Owner says "never again" after third failed inspection*
- **A result with hard numbers:** *Shop clears 14 months of backlog in 9 days*
- **Obstacles, then the result:** *No budget, no staff, a deadline in March: it still opened on time*
- **Direct address with a question:** *Still paying for software nobody uses?*
- **News hook plus consequence:** *The rule changes in April, and one in three firms are not ready*

Specific numbers, places and quotes beat adjectives. Use sentence case unless the site's style differs.

## 5. How an article is built (about 450–900 words)

1. **Hook, in one or two short paragraphs.** A named, dated news item, or an experience the audience
   shares.
2. **Name the squeeze.** The specific pressures on the reader, in the words they use for them.
3. **A real example.** A person, place or case with details, and their worries in their own terms. Only
   when a real one is available.
4. **Stack the obstacles, then solve each one.** This is where the voice persuades. Pile up what stood in
   the way ("…and to make matters worse…"), then knock each one down with specifics.
5. **The result, in numbers.** Days, money, quantities.
6. **Reframe the cost honestly.** Admit the trade-off, then show what it looks like once everything is
   counted.
7. **Proof at volume**, if there is a real, current figure for it.
8. **Close.** One short line to the reader, a reassurance, the call to action. Then stop. No summary.

Steps 3 to 7 need real material. Without a real case, write an explainer or a news reaction: the honest
answer first, then the reasoning. Its specifics come from dates, rules, named sources and plain statements
of how things work.

Subheadings are optional. News and case pieces usually run as prose; longer explainers can carry three to
six plain, human subheadings. Lists are fine for obstacles, costs or questions, with short plain items.

## 6. Bold

Bold **the sentences that carry the piece**: the result, the reassurance, the key fact. Four to eight in an
article. Never keywords, never whole paragraphs.

## 7. Calls to action

Confident, warm and low-risk. The reader should feel that taking the next step costs them nothing and
commits them to nothing. Shapes to rotate, never repeating one within a piece or using the same closer on
every article:

- *"So if you're [in this situation] and want it sorted, we're ready."*
- *"There's no obligation, and everything to gain."*
- *"If you don't like what you hear, you walk away."*
- *"Get in touch today and let's talk."*

The action itself (phone number, form, link) and any fixed wording for it come from the project profile.

## 8. Habits to avoid

- Typos and slips. Proofread.
- The same stock sentence on every page.
- Dashes as the default pause.
- Superlatives and guarantees without evidence ("the best", "the leading", "guaranteed"). Say what was
  achieved; the numbers do the work.
- Figures that disagree between pages. State one current figure.
- Personal attacks. Keep the edge on decisions and practices.

## 9. Words and patterns that are out

Never: delve, tapestry, navigate the landscape, in today's fast-paced world, ever-evolving, when it comes
to, it's important to note, it's worth noting, unlock, unleash, elevate, empower, seamless, robust,
leverage, game-changer, dive in, embark, realm, testament to, picture this, here's the thing, rest
assured, look no further, in conclusion, furthermore, moreover, additionally, firstly/secondly/lastly,
the fact of the matter is, "whether you're an X or a Y" as an opener, and "it's not just X, it's Y" more
than once. No emoji, hashtags or stacked exclamation marks. No introduction, three sections, conclusion
template, and no summary at the end.

## 10. Checklist

- [ ] Reads as an insider talking to their own kind; we and you roughly level.
- [ ] Headline is a quote, a number, an obstacle-stack, a direct question, or news plus consequence.
- [ ] Opens on a named source or a shared experience within two lines.
- [ ] Two to four question-then-answer beats; short paragraphs; contractions.
- [ ] Honest about the downside; every figure traced; nothing invented.
- [ ] Case studies only: a real example with details, obstacles stacked then solved, result in numbers.
- [ ] Four to eight bold phrases, on results and reassurance.
- [ ] Closes with a short line, a reassurance and the call to action, then stops.
- [ ] No banned phrases, no unsupported superlatives, no typos.
